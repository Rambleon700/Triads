1. What is a Triad?

A triad is simply a chord made of three different notes. Not just any three notes — they follow a specific “recipe” of intervals.

Key idea: Chords are not defined by note names (C, E, G…) but by the distances (intervals) between those notes. Change the intervals and you change the quality (major, minor, etc.).

Every triad contains:

  • Root — the note the chord is named after (the “home” note)
  • Third — a major 3rd or minor 3rd above the root (this largely determines major vs minor sound)
  • Fifth — a perfect, diminished, or augmented 5th above the root

On guitar we usually play these three notes on three adjacent strings in “close position” (notes packed as tightly as possible within an octave). These compact shapes are extremely useful for both rhythm and lead playing.

2. How Triads Are Built (Intervals on Guitar)

Intervals are measured in semitones (one fret = one semitone on the same string).

Interval Semitones Frets (same string) Sound / Role
Minor 3rd (m3) 3 3 frets Darker, sadder quality
Major 3rd (M3) 4 4 frets Bright, happy quality
Perfect 5th (P5) 7 7 frets Stable, strong foundation
Diminished 5th (♭5) 6 6 frets Tense, unstable (tritone)
Augmented 5th (#5) 8 8 frets Bright tension, outer-space feel
Tip: When notes are on different strings you still count the total semitone distance, taking into account that adjacent strings are tuned a perfect 4th (5 frets) apart (except G→B which is a major 3rd / 4 frets).

Quick Reference — Movable Major Shapes (any root)

Once you know the shape relative to the root, you can move it anywhere. The root note tells you the name of the chord.

  • Find the root on the string you want → place the corresponding shape around it.
  • The same three geometric shapes repeat for every major triad on a given string set.

8. Triad Inversions Explained

So far we have mostly shown root position (root is the lowest note). We can rearrange the same three notes so a different chord tone sits in the bass. These rearrangements are called inversions.

Position Order (low → high) Bass note Sound character
Root Position R – 3 – 5 Root Most stable, grounded
1st Inversion 3 – 5 – R Third Slightly less stable, sweeter
2nd Inversion 5 – R – 3 Fifth More “up in the air”, open

Why inversions matter:

  1. Each inversion has a subtly different emotional colour.
  2. They let you play the same chord in many places on the neck.
  3. They enable extremely smooth voice-leading (notes move only a fret or two between chords).

9. Shapes by String Set

There are four practical groups of three adjacent strings. Learning the three inversions on each set gives you complete coverage of the fretboard.

String Set Strings Common Use
Set 1 1 – 2 – 3 (e B G) Most popular for melody + rhythm; bright tone
Set 2 2 – 3 – 4 (B G D) Warm mid-range; great for chord melody
Set 3 3 – 4 – 5 (G D A) Fuller body; good for supporting vocals
Set 4 4 – 5 – 6 (D A E) Deep, powerful; less common for triads but useful
Note on the B string: Because the interval between G and B is a major 3rd (4 frets) instead of a perfect 4th (5 frets), shapes that cross the G–B strings look slightly different from shapes that stay on the other strings. Always double-check the fingering when the shape involves those two strings.

10. Connecting Triads Across the Neck

One of the biggest benefits of learning inversions is the ability to stay in one area of the neck while changing chords, or to create ascending/descending lines that outline the harmony.

Example: G major → G minor (same area)

Simply lower the 3rd (B → B♭) in any G major shape. The rest of the notes stay put.

Example: Moving a C major triad up the neck

Root position → 1st inversion → 2nd inversion → root position (one octave higher). The top note climbs: G → C → E → G…

Practice idea: Pick one chord (e.g. D major) and play all three inversions on strings 1-2-3, then move to strings 2-3-4 and repeat. Do the same for the minor version. This builds both muscle memory and fretboard knowledge.

11. Smooth Chord Progressions with Triads

Because inversions let you keep common tones and move other voices by only a fret or two, triad progressions can sound much smoother than jumping between big open or barre chords.

Using close-position triads on the top three or four strings, each note typically moves by only one or two frets. The result is a flowing, connected sound that is perfect for intros, interludes, or fingerstyle arrangements.

Try this yourself

  • Take a simple two-chord vamp (Am – F) and find as many ways as possible to switch between them using only triads on strings 1-2-3.
  • Play a I–vi–IV–V progression in C (C – Am – F – G) staying roughly between frets 5–10.
  • Arpeggiate the triads instead of strumming them — instant melodic material that perfectly outlines the harmony.

12. Practice Tips & Next Steps

Recommended practice path

  1. Master the four qualities in root position on one string set (start with strings 1-2-3).
  2. Add the two inversions so you have three shapes per quality.
  3. Move the shapes to different roots (C, G, D, A, E, F…).
  4. Connect inversions up and down the neck for a single chord.
  5. Apply to progressions — focus on minimal movement between chords.
  6. Expand to other string sets once the first set feels automatic.
Ear training bonus: Play a random triad shape and try to identify its quality (major/minor/dim/aug) and which inversion it is by ear before looking at your fretting hand. This dramatically improves both theory and improvisation.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Learning shapes without knowing which note is the root — always be able to point to the root.
  • Only practising in one area of the neck.
  • Ignoring the sound of each inversion (they are not interchangeable emotionally).
  • Forgetting that the G–B string interval is different — shapes that cross it need a slight adjustment.

Remember: Knowing the theory is only the first step. The real power of triads appears when the shapes become automatic and you can grab the right inversion instantly for voice-leading, fills, or soloing. Spend time just playing and listening — the fretboard will start to make sense in a whole new way.

Teacher Resource

Teaching Guitar Triads

Ready-to-use lesson ideas that turn the interactive triad mapper into clear, memorable student experiences.

Duration: 45–60 min (or 20 min mini-version) Level: Beginner → Intermediate Materials: This HTML file (projected or on devices), guitars, optional phone for recording

Lesson Goals

Pick 1–2 per session so the lesson stays focused:

  • Understand what a triad is and how the four qualities are built
  • See and hear the difference between major and minor
  • Locate any major/minor triad anywhere on the neck
  • Use inversions for smooth chord changes
  • Connect open chords to the smaller triad shapes

Suggested Lesson Flow (45–60 min)

0–5 min · Warm-up

Context from songs they know

Play a simple progression students already know (e.g. G–Em–C–D or Am–F–C–G). Ask:

  • “These chords are all made of only three notes. Can you hear that?”
  • “What changes when we go from G to Em?”
5–15 min · Concept

What is a triad? The four qualities

Open the Guide page to “What is a Triad?” and “The Four Qualities”. Keep it short and visual:

  • Root + 3rd + 5th
  • Major = bright (R – M3 – P5)
  • Minor = darker (R – m3 – P5)

Point out the colour legend: green = root, blue/purple = 3rd, amber = 5th. Students should be able to point to each colour and name its role.

15–35 min · Interactive discovery

Use the Triad Mapper (the strongest part)

Project the tool or have students open it on phones/tablets.

  1. Choose a familiar open chord (start with C major or A minor).
  2. Look at the full fretboard together. Ask:
    • “Where is the root on the low E string?”
    • “How many places can you find the same three notes?”
  3. Switch Major ↔ Minor and watch the 3rd move. Students hear and see the difference immediately.
  4. Call-out game: students name a root (“D major!” / “F# minor!”). Race to find the nearest shape on strings 1-2-3.
Teacher tip: Stay on the top three strings at first. The full neck can overwhelm beginners.
35–50 min · Playing application

Smooth voice-leading

Give a simple two-chord vamp (Am–F or C–G). Rules:

  • Stay on the top three strings only
  • Change chords by moving to the nearest inversion
  • Goal: almost no jumping — notes move 1–2 frets max

Optional upgrade: arpeggiate the triads instead of strumming. Instant melodic material that perfectly outlines the harmony.

50–60 min · Cool-down & take-home

Personal mapping task

Each student picks one chord they struggle with, maps it in the tool, and writes down three places they can play it between frets 0–12. Share one discovery with a partner before leaving.

Differentiation

Beginner

Only major + minor. Only strings 1-2-3. Only root position first. Link every shape back to an open chord they already know.

Intermediate

All three inversions. Move between two chords with smooth voice leading. Introduce a second string set (2-3-4).

Advanced

Full neck. Add diminished/augmented later. Create a 4-bar progression using only triads. Solo by arpeggiating the shapes.

Classroom Activities & Games

GAME Find the Root

Point to any green note on the projected fretboard. Students call out the string and fret. Builds instant root awareness.

GAME Call-and-Response

You name a chord + inversion (“A minor, first inversion”). Students have 5 seconds to play it. Increase speed as they improve.

EAR Quality Switch

Play a triad shape, then lower or raise the 3rd. Students identify major vs minor by ear before looking at their hands.

CREATIVE Record the Smooth Version

Have a student (or the group) play Am–F–C–G using only top-three-string triads. Record on a phone. Compare to the open-chord version. Very motivating.

20-Minute Mini-Lesson

When you only have a short slot:

  1. Open the tool → C major
  2. Students play the three highest notes of open C
  3. Switch the tool to C minor → students lower the 3rd
  4. Find the same C major triad around frets 8–10
  5. Play C – Am – F – G using only top-three-string triads

That’s usually enough for one solid, memorable session.

Common Pitfalls to Watch For

  • Students memorise shapes without knowing which note is the root — always ask them to point to the green note.
  • Jumping straight to the whole neck. Stay on one string set until it feels automatic.
  • Ignoring the sound of inversions. Have them play root position vs 1st vs 2nd and describe the difference.
  • Forgetting the G–B string quirk (major 3rd instead of perfect 4th). Flag it early when shapes cross those strings.
